# 📌 MASTER SUMMARY — Doing Church Together, Churchwide Formation, and Church Intelligence

Prepared for Brett Eastman · September 15, 2026

This document consolidates recovered source material and the direction Brett expressed in this conversation. ## 1. The central idea

**Working thesis: We can help the whole church learn to live its faith together by connecting what is preached with what is practiced—in worship, groups, homes, daily life, ministry, and mission.**

The opportunity is to make alignment a recurring, developmental practice. A campaign can create shared attention and action; the next task is to sustain relationships, develop hosts and leaders, and carry those practices into the next season. Small groups are an essential environment for this work, alongside families, ministry teams, and participation in the wider community.

Brett’s latest emphasis matters: **“We have to think differently.”** The “we” expresses the method as well as the message. The work should equip people to contribute, learn, serve, and lead together. Technology can support preparation and coordination; the intended result is more human relationship and faithful action.

## 2. Naming recommendation — proposed, not final

**Flagship book and pastoral invitation: Doing Church Together**

**Working subtitle: A Practical Guide to Aligning Your Church, Equipping Your People, and Reaching Your Community**

**Name of the approach: Churchwide Formation**

Use “churchwide formation” when describing the distinctive approach. “Church formation” remains a useful broader category and is already present in existing files, but can sound like forming or organizing a church. “Churchwide” makes participation across the whole congregation explicit. “Together” supplies the relational invitation and connects naturally to LifeTogether.

Give “wide” a concrete meaning:

- Across ministries: shared mission, values, formation priorities, and practical coordination.
- Across generations: preschool, elementary, students, adults, parents, and grandparents.
- Across the week: worship gatherings, groups, the family table, car rides, bedtime, work, and service.
- Across the community: members, neighbors, and people the church is called to serve.
- Across sources of wisdom: local teaching enriched by carefully evaluated learning from other churches and traditions appropriate to the church’s convictions and context.

“Wide” should describe reach and participation, while formation describes depth. Do not make the word responsible for explaining every feature of AI. Technology belongs inside the practical method; the title promises a human and pastoral outcome.

**The test:** Does this help people follow Christ, belong to one another, and participate in the church’s mission? Agreement with a senior pastor is not a sufficient measure of formation. Christ remains the Shepherd; pastors and ministry leaders serve under him. Alignment should preserve appropriate theological accountability, age-appropriate teaching, pastoral judgment, and space for questions.

Keep **This Changes Everything** as a possible campaign or section title. It communicates energy but gives a pastor less immediate information about the subject. A series of “Doing … Together” books could eventually work, but build recognition around the flagship before multiplying titles. Brand availability, trademarks, and market response have not been investigated here.

Preserve both meanings as distinct layers: **local church knowledge** and **evaluated external learning**. External material must not silently override a congregation’s convictions or be presented as its pastor’s own words.

## 5. Family formation belongs in the first version

Brett’s instruction in this conversation: program for families’ actual lives, rather than assuming they can remain for multiple Sunday services. No population percentage has been verified for that observation.

Produce three weekly Family Conversations editions: preschool, elementary, and youth. Give parents a concise summary of what their children actually heard, a small set of useful questions, and one practical response. When the adult and age-group teaching differ, represent those differences faithfully while connecting them to shared formation priorities. Do not substitute an invented youth message derived from the adult sermon.

Use the table, car ride, bedtime, and ordinary in-between moments as opportunities. Include couples, grandparents, and different household circumstances. Avoid turning every conversation into another assignment. Family formation should connect households with actual community and support, including small groups and other families.

## 6. The five purposes and the five environments

Keep the five biblical purposes as a theological and practical foundation: worship, fellowship, discipleship, ministry, and mission/evangelism, using the language appropriate to the church. Churchwide formation is a broader implementation scope, not necessarily a claim to a sixth purpose or a better theology.

The purposes answer **what the church is cultivating**. The environments answer **where people practice it**. Intelligence areas answer **what needs to be understood and supported**. A 50-category curriculum catalog answers **what subjects might be addressed**. These are different organizing systems; do not collapse them into one list.

A practical design should demonstrate ministry and mission as well as learning, worship, and fellowship. Each shared series can include an act of service, a relational invitation, or a community partnership appropriate to the congregation. Sharing sermon content alone does not establish whole-church formation.

## 7. A five-year legacy strategy — recommendation

Brett’s five-year horizon is treated as a planning constraint, not a medical claim or prediction.

**Year 1: Make the teaching transferable.** Choose the flagship promise, capture firsthand stories, write a useful book and playbook, and test one series in which adults, groups, families, and leaders have coordinated resources. Record the actual time required and the pastoral work it supports.

**Year 2: Make the process repeatable.** Repeat the model in another season and in churches with different sizes and staffing. Test three recurring workflows: sermon-to-group resources, teaching-to-family conversations, and leader coaching. Improve the process from evidence before adding more products.

**Year 3: Make the system support others.** Build the shared platform around practices that proved useful. Establish source permissions, pastoral review, age adaptation, production templates, and a manageable annual rhythm. Enable distinct ministry applications without duplicating the underlying system.

**Year 4: Make the leadership transferable.** Train coaches, editors, ministry leaders, and successor operators. Document how decisions are made and how churches preserve their own convictions. Ensure delivery does not require Brett to attend every meeting or approve every routine item.

**Year 5: Make the work durable.** Transfer operating knowledge, rights documentation, source stewardship, and leadership responsibilities. Publish tested practices and credible stories of their results. Leave a teachable method, a capable team, and maintainable resources.

The aim is a body of work other people can carry forward. Multiple platforms become valuable when they extend that method and its relationships.

## Recommended publishing structure

Use the function-based outline below for the flagship book. It invites a senior pastor into a pastoral outcome without requiring interest in technology or a new vocabulary. Use the intelligence-based outline for the companion team experience:

**The 21-Day Churchwide Intelligence Challenge**

**Working subtitle: A Practical Journey for Pastors and Teams to Connect Teaching, Families, Groups, and Mission**

These serve two reading situations: a book that develops conviction and a staff workbook that turns that conviction into decisions and practical work. They can share stories, frameworks, and templates without becoming identical books with different covers.

Churchwide Formation names the approach. Church Intelligence supports its sources, context, coordination, and learning. LifeTogether remains the parent identity. Sermon Curator and Small Group Ministry Intelligence can become specialist playbooks and applications within this structure.

## The central thesis

**The whole church can participate in formation when its teaching, relationships, households, ministries, and mission are intentionally connected. Pastors and teams can cultivate that connection through shared direction, daily conversations, practical action, and the development of other leaders.**

The personal invitation is “we have to think differently.” The practical question is what people can do together that remains difficult when every leader, household, or ministry works in isolation. The book should demonstrate cooperation through its stories, contributors, ministry examples, and implementation process.

This is an invitation to renewal. A comparison with historic reform movements may inspire the ambition, but should not substitute for a precise claim or imply that a publishing strategy has the theological significance of the Reformation.

## Twenty-one proposed theses

1. Christ is the Shepherd; every local pastoral leader serves under his authority.
2. Churchwide formation joins proclamation to the ordinary practices of following Christ together.
3. Shared mission and values need recurring expression in decisions, relationships, and ministry activity.
4. Alignment is strongest when people understand the purpose and contribute to its expression.
5. A congregation’s unity can include many trusted teachers and ministry leaders.
6. Sunday teaching can begin conversations that continue throughout the week.
7. Every generation needs developmentally appropriate ways to participate.
8. Parents and caregivers need usable help for the moments already present in family life.
9. People without a participating household also need accessible pathways into community.
10. Small groups can become places of application, mutual care, service, and leader development.
11. Ministry teams can practice formation while carrying out their responsibilities.
12. Worship, fellowship, discipleship, ministry, and mission belong together in the church’s life.
13. A campaign should include a continuation pathway before it launches.
14. Daily resources should invite reflection, conversation, and action rather than stop at content delivery.
15. People should have meaningful print and digital participation options.
16. Congregation members’ experiences and questions can inform pastoral understanding and follow-up.
17. Local teaching can be enriched through carefully evaluated learning from the wider church.
18. AI-assisted resources require accountable source selection, review, and pastoral judgment.
19. Tools and systems should help people make room for ministry, relationships, and rest.
20. Multiplication depends on developing people who can lead and equip others.
21. A durable legacy includes transferable practices, entrusted leadership, and resources others can maintain.

These are editorial propositions for the book, not attributed quotations or a completed biblical argument. Each chapter must develop its proposition from appropriate Scripture, verified source material, and concrete ministry experience.

## How the language of intelligence belongs in the book

Use the terms only when they describe a demonstrable practice:

- **Actual:** grounded in real source materials and observable ministry experience.
- **Authentic:** faithful to the church’s convictions and to what a source actually says.
- **Approved:** reviewed and authorized by the people accountable for the material.
- **Aligned:** connected with the church’s mission, values, audience, and context.
- **Accelerated:** preparation made faster where evidence supports the claim, with quality preserved.

These are standards for the work. They are not reasons to relabel AI as something other than AI or to assume its output is correct. Define ordinary ministry intelligence as understanding people, sources, context, and practice well enough to make responsible decisions.

# Churchwide Formation: Weekly Conversations and Focused Campaigns

Design brief for Brett Eastman · September 15, 2026

Companion to Churchwide-Formation-Master-Summary.md. This captures Brett’s latest direction and separates it from proposed implementation details. It is a resource and platform specification, not a finished devotional manuscript or a published Lulu product.

## Purpose and audience

Help people carry the church’s teaching into daily relationships and action. Serve individuals, families, small groups, and ministry teams across generations, under shared pastoral direction and with age-appropriate applications.

The core promise: **Every sermon begins a week of conversations, practices, and opportunities to respond.** Focused 21-, 30-, and 40-day experiences build on this ongoing rhythm.

## Direction supplied by Brett

- Provide sermon-connected daily devotions and conversations every week, including weeks without a coordinated campaign across age-group ministries.
- Make family, small-group, ministry, and generational applications central to the design.
- When producing companion video devotions, include multiple ministry leaders. Interpret the dictated “902nd” as approximately 90 seconds.
- Include an open question that invites a story, application, or next step. Make it possible for the senior pastor and staff to see responses people choose to submit.
- Create focused 21-, 30-, and 40-day editions with a distinctive purpose and progression.
- Design for physical and digital participation from the beginning, with space to write in the printed devotional or workbook.
- Use Lulu as the intended print-on-demand route. Churches may distribute books, subsidize them, or sell them; the church chooses its approach.
- Include small-group conversation editions for 30- and 40-day experiences. For major campaigns, plan a youth edition and, where feasible, a children’s edition.

## The weekly baseline — proposed implementation

The Sunday message provides the source anchor. Every week has seven touchpoints: a Sunday response and six Monday–Saturday devotions. This is an editorial rhythm, not a claim that any duration guarantees habit formation.

| Touchpoint | Purpose | Example type of response |
|---|---|---|
| Sunday | Receive the message and identify its invitation | What stayed with you, and what question are you carrying? |
| Monday | Recall a central biblical truth | Where does this connect with your life today? |
| Tuesday | Reflect honestly | What makes this difficult for you? |
| Wednesday | Discuss with another person | What could you learn from each other’s experience? |
| Thursday | Practice a concrete response | What small action could you take? |
| Friday | Extend the practice through service or invitation | Who might benefit from your response? |
| Saturday | Reflect, share, and prepare to continue | What happened, and what remains unfinished? |

These are generic editorial prompts, not adaptations of a particular sermon. The actual source determines the week’s sequence and applications. Do not force a sermon into a formula that changes its meaning.

Each daily resource contains a Scripture reference, a brief source-faithful reflection, one open conversation question, a practical invitation, and room for a written response or prayer. Stories appear when real, relevant, and available with permission; do not invent testimony to fill a template.

A shared daily core can support short audience-specific prompts for preschool families, elementary families, youth and their families, adult groups, and ministry teams. Include an individual reflection route for people without a household or group available. Grandparents and other trusted adults can participate where appropriate.

If youth or children studied a different passage on Sunday, identify that accurately. Parents can receive both a faithful summary of their child’s actual teaching and a connection to the church’s shared formation priorities. Shared direction does not require pretending every ministry heard the adult sermon.

## Companion videos: shared leadership and a clear invitation

The senior pastor establishes direction. Youth leaders, children’s leaders, small-group leaders, worship leaders, care leaders, volunteers, and congregation members can contribute relevant applications under the church’s review process. This is shared leadership in service of a coherent message.

Proposed 90-second outline:

1. About 15 seconds: reconnect with the message and biblical focus.
2. About 35 seconds: offer one practical explanation or a brief, verified experience.
3. About 25 seconds: invite a realistic action and ask one open question.
4. About 15 seconds: offer a short prayer or explain how to share a response.

Treat timing as a production aid. Start with a sustainable recording cadence; the daily written conversation remains available whether or not every day has a video. Provide captions and a readable equivalent so video is optional for participation.

## The response and pastoral learning loop

The intended sequence is: teaching → daily reflection → conversation → practice → voluntary response → staff learning and pastoral follow-up.

Give people different response routes: private journaling, conversation with their household or group, a paper response card, or a digital submission. Writing in a journal does not make that writing available to staff. A QR code and short URL can connect a printed resource to the optional response form and video.

Distinguish a private reflection, a request for pastoral contact, and a story offered for possible public sharing. Ask permission before public reuse. Route children’s participation through appropriate parent and ministry processes. Do not use an open public feed for sensitive pastoral responses.

A designated staff member reviews submissions, routes care requests, and summarizes recurring themes for the senior pastor and staff. Include questions and difficulties as well as encouraging stories. A response form should not imply that every submission will receive a personal reply unless the church has staffing to deliver that.

## Focused editions

| Edition | Intended emphasis | Companion resources |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | Apply the current sermon in ordinary life | Sunday reflection, six daily devotions, family prompts, one group conversation guide, ministry application, optional leader videos |
| 21-day | Practice one clearly defined response with a beginning and an end | Daily journal, weekly conversations, leader support, closing reflection and next step |
| 30-day | Develop a focused theme through reflection, practice, and relationships | Print/digital devotional, small-group conversation edition, facilitator notes, family prompts, next-step pathway |
| 40-day | Bring the congregation into a coordinated season of learning and action | Devotional/workbook, sermon support, small-group edition, host coaching, ministry and mission opportunities, testimony capture, continuation plan |
| Major campaign age editions | Help young people participate meaningfully | Youth edition planned as a priority; children’s edition where feasible; developmentally appropriate activities and parent support |

Do not pad a source to reach a duration. Use the exact calendar to determine sessions and daily entries. Keep one shared transformation objective while adapting vocabulary, examples, activities, and questions by audience.

The campaign answers seven practical questions: What is the message? What brings people into the shared experience? Who champions it? How does each person receive resources? How are stories invited? How are hosts and leaders developed? What will people do after the final day?

## Print and digital from the beginning

Design a readable digital edition and a print layout with generous writing space from the same reviewed content. A hardcover devotional journal is an intended option; also evaluate a writable paperback/workbook format for cost and everyday use. Do not claim that hardcover or handwriting produces greater spiritual transformation for everyone. The design rationale is choice, accessibility, reflection, and participation.

Lulu supports print-on-demand book production. Its current guidance separates production from shipping; production generally takes 3–5 business days and estimates are not guaranteed. Therefore, a book based on an already-delivered Sunday sermon cannot be assumed to arrive for Monday use.

Proposed production solution:

- **Weekly response:** release the reviewed digital resource and a locally printable handout. If the final sermon arrives late, publish after review rather than presenting an unreviewed draft as approved teaching.
- **Campaign book:** develop from the approved series plan well before launch, review a physical proof, and allow production and shipping time. Sunday-specific stories and changed applications can appear in a digital companion or printed insert.
- **Later collection:** consider a bound collection of completed weekly devotions, preserving the actual teaching and approved stories after the series.

Lulu requires finished print files, including an interior PDF and a suitable cover. Final specifications, page count, proofing, delivery estimates, and landed cost must be established for each edition. Brett’s “$10 on the patio” is an example of church-controlled distribution, not a verified viable retail price for every format, particularly hardcover.

- [Lulu Direct: production and shipping](https://help.luludirect.lulu.com/en/support/solutions/articles/64000311855-how-do-lulu-direct-orders-work-)

|---|---|---|
| Establish teaching and church alignment | Senior pastor or designated theological reviewer | Approved source, biblical focus, formation objective |
| Adapt daily and group resources | Editor and group-ministry leader | Source-linked daily entries and conversation guide |

AI may assist transcription, source organization, drafts, adaptations, and preparation of staff summaries. Human reviewers remain accountable for theology, source accuracy, age suitability, and decisions about pastoral responses. This is a design requirement, not a description of a platform already implemented.

## Why shared pastoral direction may not reach the whole church

The problem is not always that people cannot hear the sermon. The church may lack a practical way to connect its teaching with ministry decisions, household conversations, leader development, and service. Diagnosis should precede a demand for greater conformity.

| Possible problem | How to recognize it | Practical response |
|---|---|---|
| Direction is too abstract | Leaders can repeat the vision but describe different priorities | Translate each value into a few observable practices and shared seasonal objectives |
| Planning comes too late | Groups and age ministries receive sermon information after preparing their own resources | Share the series direction early, with a clear process for changes and final review |
| Staff operate with disconnected calendars | Good ministries compete for the same volunteers and families | Maintain one shared formation calendar and resolve conflicts together |
| The pastor is the only interpreter | Resources wait for the senior pastor to write or film everything | Equip other leaders to explain and apply the teaching within agreed convictions and review responsibilities |
| Resources do not fit the audience | An adult discussion guide is merely simplified for young children | Give age-ministry leaders responsibility for developmental adaptation and accurate parent communication |
| Group leaders lack support | Outside curriculum fills a practical gap the church has left open | Supply useful material and coaching; invite leaders into planning and retain appropriate exceptions |
| The sermon has no clear follow-up | People remember a point but do not know what to do with it | Provide daily reflection, an open question, a practical response, and a community connection |
| Participation assumes ideal availability | Families need long Sunday attendance or heavy homework to take part | Offer brief household conversations, flexible group participation, print and digital access |
| Nobody listens to the response | Staff count distribution and views but miss questions and lived experience | Invite voluntary responses, route care needs, and discuss what participants are learning |
| Trust is strained | Leaders comply publicly but disengage privately | Listen to concerns and repair relationships; more content cannot resolve a trust problem |

The benefit of shared direction is repeated opportunity to understand and practice the church’s mission. It can reduce conflicting priorities, make leader support reusable, help families talk across generations, and make community participation easier to navigate. These are intended benefits to test. They do not justify treating every independent study or different emphasis as disloyalty.

## What the campaign experience contributes

The transferable design elements are a clear beginning, a shared biblical focus, a manageable commitment, coordinated resources, host and leader preparation, multiple participation settings, a practical response, and a visible next step.

Treat a campaign as a period of concentrated participation within an ongoing formation rhythm. The weekly foundation continues before and after it: sermon follow-up, daily conversations, family prompts, group support, ministry application, and pastoral learning from voluntary responses.

The proposed repeat pattern is one initial experience, followed by a second four to six months later if the first is useful and sustainable. Between them, strengthen relationships and leaders. This interval reflects Brett’s stated interest and a suggested implementation rhythm, not a measured national norm.

## Ten ways to invite a senior pastor into the work

1. **Show one actual week.** Use an authorized sermon to demonstrate a devotional, group conversation, family prompt, and leader-video outline. Let the pastor evaluate source fidelity and usefulness.
2. **Ask what should still be happening on Thursday.** Start with the intended response to the message, then design practical support for that response.
3. **Begin with an existing series.** Help the church strengthen work it already plans to do, with a clearly stated additional workload.
4. **Put a family’s experience in view.** Show how the parent of a preschooler, elementary child, and student can ask a useful question about that child’s actual teaching.
5. **Invite ministry leaders to contribute.** Record brief applications from several leaders under shared direction, showing how the model develops the team.
6. **Bring a physical journal to the conversation.** Demonstrate the writing space, daily prompt, and optional connection to video and response tools.
7. **Start with a mission opportunity.** Build a shared series around a local service or relational invitation that ministries can participate in together.
8. **Make the continuation visible.** Present the next study, host support, and ministry pathway alongside the campaign launch plan.
9. **Make the tradeoff measurable.** Track preparation and coordination time alongside actual use. A tool that saves writing time but adds more review work has not necessarily improved capacity.
10. **Let the church’s own evidence speak.** Share permissioned stories, questions, continuation rates, and practical changes from its first experience before proposing expansion.

These invitations should be adapted to the pastor’s gifts, church tradition, team capacity, and priorities. The offer is assistance with their ministry responsibility, not a judgment that they have failed by focusing on the weekend.
